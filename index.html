The making of high-tech clusters: Evidence from the American silicon breakthrough

Abstract

One of the largest technology opportunities of the past century was the emergence of the planar process for the manufacturing of silicon-based semiconductors. In this paper, I examine how the U.S. semiconductor firms concentrated their R&D activities across space to exploit this opportunity, a process that spurred new high-tech clusters in 1960-2000s. Using a new digitized and geocoded dataset on semiconductor firms' patents, products, production capacity, and engineering workforce at the firm-laboratory level, I show that firm R&D sites with history in silicon-based R&D agglomerated innovation, production, and commercial effort in pursuit of this opportunity. Engineers at these sites were exposed to the complete R&D value chain, and were more likely to be promoted as well as to be poached by competing firms. In the short run, firms were more likely to concentrate their activities in selected sites with both competence to exploit the silicon technology and few nearby competitors. Over time, these new R&D locations attracted new entrants, contributing to the emergence of new clusters.
